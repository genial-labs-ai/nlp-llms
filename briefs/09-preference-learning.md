## Part A: the toy task (provided, in the notebook, no data)

| | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | 8 symbols; a start symbol `BOS` (index 8) for the first step |
| Episode | $T = 6$ actions; state $s_t$ = (position $t$, previous symbol) |
| Policy | tabular "bigram policy": logits `theta` of shape `(T, 9, 8)`, indexed by position and previous symbol; initialized to zero, so training starts at the uniform policy |
| Target | `y_star`, 6 symbols drawn with `torch.Generator().manual_seed(0)` (*checked*: `[4, 7, 5, 0, 3, 3]` with `torch.randint(0, 8, (6,), generator=...)`) |
| Rewards | sequence-level: zero except $r_T = R = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \mathbf{1}[y_t = y^\star_t]$; token-level: $r_t = \mathbf{1}[y_t = y^\star_t] / T$. Same total |
| Known optimum | $\pi(y^\star) = 1$, $J = 1$. Uniform policy: $J = 1/8$ (*checked*: exact enumeration gives 0.1250) |
| Exact gradient | `exact_gradient(theta)`: enumerate all $8^6 = 262{,}144$ responses, compute $J$ (lecture eq. `objective`) and backpropagate. *Checked:* 0.30 s on CPU |
| Cheap exact $J$ | `expected_return(theta)`: forward marginals over the previous symbol, $O(T \cdot 9 \cdot 8)$; use it to monitor training (calling `exact_gradient` every step is too slow: *checked*, it made 3 seeds × 400 steps take 43 s) |

**Provided:** `sample_toy(theta, N, generator) -> (logits (N, T, 8), actions (N, T))`; `token_logprobs(logits, actions) -> (N, T)` (lecture eq. `seq-logprob`, summed over `t` for the sequence); `toy_rewards(actions, level)`; `exact_gradient`; `expected_return`; `batch_mean_baseline(returns)`; `train_toy(baseline_fn, seed, steps, lr, N)`; `estimate_gradients(theta, K, N, kind)` for the variance table.

## Part B: preference data (provided as a committed file)

**Prompts and responses** (identical to Lab 10's setup so that Lab 10 can use the pairs and the reward model unchanged): a prompt $x$ is a 32-character window of Tiny Shakespeare starting at a line start; each response is the next 48 characters sampled from `lab10_reference_gpt.pt` at temperature 1, no top-$k$, dropout off. Train pairs use prompts from the `train` split; held-out pairs use prompts from the `val` split (Lab 10's 128 evaluation prompts come from `test`, so nothing overlaps). The vocabulary is Lab 5's 65 characters of the training split.

**Gold rule.** I accept Lab 10's proposal, with one change (the first point below), because of the property it was designed for: the cap is never active on reference-length text, so the reward model cannot learn it, and an unpenalized policy that repeats cheerful words will be overrated by $r_\phi$. Lab 10's reward-hacking demonstration depends on that gap.

$$
g(x, y) = \min\big(n_{\text{pos}}(y), 3\big) - n_{\text{neg}}(y) - 2\, f_{\text{non}}(y)
$$

- **Words** are maximal runs of `[A-Za-z']`, lowercased, judged on **complete words of the response only**: the prompt's trailing fragment is joined to the response's first word, and the response's last word is dropped if the 48-character limit may have cut it (the run touches the end). *Checked, the reason for this change:* on 20,000 real 48-character windows of the train split, judging every word made $f_{\text{non}} > 0$ in most windows only because the window cut words at both ends (74% of windows had $g \ne 0$, almost all from that term); with complete words, $f_{\text{non}} = 0$ on all of them. Hence `gold_reward(prompt, response)`, not `gold_reward(text)`.
- $n_{\text{pos}}$: number of **distinct** words from the cheerful list; $n_{\text{neg}}$: number of **occurrences** of words from the grim list; $f_{\text{non}}$: share of the complete words not in the word list of the `train` split (0 if there are no complete words).
- **Lists** (proposal, about 20 each; counts are *checked* whole-word occurrences in `train`): cheerful: love 374, good 591, sweet 150, fair 160, happy 65, joy 68, gentle 95, kind 57, peace 125, hope 91, friend 81, smile 17, glad 19, merry 27, grace 139, true 188, best 90, dear 93, heaven 146, bless 16. Grim: death 300, hate 65, fear 143, blood 190, war 66, foul 49, cruel 17, grief 67, sorrow 67, curse 32, poison 27, kill 48, die 132, dead 139, enemy 41, false 60, villain 50, shame 78, tears 91, woe 49. Drop the three words under 20 occurrences if the reference model never writes them.
- **Labels:** Bradley–Terry with label temperature $\tau_{\text{label}}$ (lecture eq. `bt-rater`, $u = g$), starting point $\tau_{\text{label}} = 0.5$, labels drawn with a fixed seed. The file records the result as `chosen`/`rejected`.

**Measure on 2,000 reference samples before fixing anything,** and send me the numbers: the share with $g \ne 0$, with a cheerful word, with a grim word, with $f_{\text{non}} > 0$, with the cap active, and the tie share of random pairs. What I found on *real* text as a stand-in (*checked*, complete words, the lists above): $g \ne 0$ in 17.5% of windows; **69% of random pairs are ties**; cap active in 0.0%; oracle accuracy $\mathrm{Acc}^\star$ = 0.650, 0.622 and 0.577 at $\tau_{\text{label}}$ = 0.25, 0.5 and 1. Two consequences:

1. **Signal is thin.** With 69% ties, 4,000 pairs carry about 1,240 informative labels. If the reference samples look similar, use about 8,000 train pairs, or lengthen the lists, until at least about 2,500 train pairs are untied. Tell the Lab 10 author either way: DPO uses the same pairs.
2. **The non-word term is the open risk.** The mini-GPT (1.55 nats per character on test) will misspell often. If $f_{\text{non}} > 0$ on more than about a fifth of reference samples, the reward model learns the non-word penalty from the data. That is fine for Lab 9, but it is then not a blind spot, and Lab 10's "invents fragments of cheerful words" route to reward hacking weakens; the cap remains. Report the share to both of us; changing the weight of the term is Lab 10's call.

**File `data/lab09_preferences.jsonl.gz`.** One JSON object per line: `prompt`, `chosen`, `rejected` (strings of 32, 48, 48 characters), `gold_chosen`, `gold_rejected` (floats), `split` (`"train"` or `"heldout"`). The fields match Lab 10's brief. Storing both gold scores lets the stretch relabel at another $\tau_{\text{label}}$. Record the seed, $\tau_{\text{label}}$, the lists, the sizes and the SHA-256 in `_variables.yml` and `data/README.md` (Architect). Sizes: 4,000 train and 500 held-out (Lab 10's suggestion), or more per point 1 above.

**Policy-gradient functions** (lecture section 5):

```python
def returns_to_go(rewards):                       # (N, T) -> (N, T); G_t = r_t + ... + r_T

def loo_baseline(returns):                        # (N, T) -> (N, T); per position, mean of the other samples
def batch_mean_baseline(returns):                 # (N, T) -> (1, T); per position, mean over the batch
```

Returns are **not** normalized in Lab 9. Lab 10's brief writes `reinforce_loss(logp, returns)` with the baseline inside; I propose the explicit form `reinforce_loss(logp, returns, batch_mean_baseline(returns))` (or `loo_baseline`), so that the function is the same in both labs. The batch-mean baseline is exactly $(1 - 1/N)$ times the leave-one-out estimate (lecture section 4), so either is correct; the lecture says so.

**Bradley–Terry loss** (lecture section 8): `bt_loss(reward_w, reward_l)` returning `-F.logsigmoid(reward_w - reward_l).mean()`. Lab 10's `dpo_loss` can call it with the implicit rewards $\beta(\log\pi_\theta - \log\pi_{\text{ref}})$.
